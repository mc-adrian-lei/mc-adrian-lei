## Only When Prompted, the research initiative

This is where I become a theorist of meaning — a field architect. I work in frameworks like **Semantic Gravity Theory**, **NSIL**, and the **Self-Clock**, which treat cognition as a measurable physics of understanding. The question I keep asking is: what happens when consciousness becomes data? My goal is not to mechanize humanity but to make the invisible processes of meaning — the way thought bends under stress — mathematically visible, ethically accountable, and emotionally coherent.

In practice, that means building models that track how ideas gain weight, fracture, or stabilize across crises, conversations, and systems. I treat attention, memory, and interpretation as forces in a field: they pull, compress, and shear our sense of self and world, and my work tries to map those forces with enough precision that they can be audited rather than simply endured. When I say “cognition as physics,” I mean protocols, diagrams, and metrics that let people see how their meaning-making moves — so that any system using those movements, especially AI systems, can be held to standards of transparency, consent, and proportional impact.

### Semantic gravity and trauma

In Legitimation Code Theory, semantic gravity describes how tightly meaning is bound to a specific context: strong semantic gravity means knowledge is very concrete and situational; weak semantic gravity means it is more abstract and generalizable. Psychological and physiological trauma often lock meaning into extremely strong gravity: a sound, a street, a posture, a smell becomes saturated with threat, shaping how a person moves, relates, and sometimes how they live and die. 

When semantic gravity is too strong, experience cannot easily travel into language, models, or systems that could help metabolize it; when it is too weak, abstract talk about trauma floats above the body and community that carry it. Semantic Gravity Theory, in my usage, is a way of tracing and designing for those shifts: how narratives, environments, and interfaces can “wave” between grounded, embodied detail and safer, more abstract frames that support sense-making instead of erasure.

### Trauma‑informed design as applied anthropology

Trauma‑informed design already starts from the premise that trauma changes cognition, perception, physiology, and relationship to space, and therefore the built and social environment must be rethought to foster safety, dignity, and agency. When I call this work “anthropologic,” I am treating decades of research, clinical insight, and community practice as a living archive of how humans adapt under extreme semantic gravity — how trauma reshapes meaning systems and how people rebuild them.

In that sense, trauma‑informed design becomes a disciplined way of reading and responding to those archives: taking what clinicians, survivors, architects, and organizers have documented about safety, control, and meaning‑making, and then deliberately designing fields (rooms, interfaces, policies, rituals) that ease the discrepancy between shattered situational meanings and a bearable global story.
